# English Presentation Script — Master's Research Report
## TOEFL ~70 Level · ~5 Minutes · Slide-by-Slide

---

### Slide 01 — Cover (Title Slide)

Good morning, everyone. Today I will present my research about how to design better signs for plant gardens. I did this study at Fukuoka City Botanical Garden in Japan. I used eye-tracking to test if new sign designs work better than old ones.

---

### Slide 02 — Problem 1: Knowledge Overload

The first problem is simple. Many signs in plant gardens have too much difficult information. Visitors try to read, but they feel tired very quickly. Within just 10 seconds, most people give up and stop reading. The signs have too many hard words.

---

### Slide 03 — Problem 2: Weak Evaluation Methods

The second problem is about testing. In the past, we only used questionnaires and interviews to check if signs are good. But these methods are not accurate enough. We could not see where visitors actually look. We needed a better way to measure.

---

### Slide 04 — Research Question 1

So my first research question is: Can a new sign design help people read more text in the first 10 seconds? I wanted to see if visitors spend more time on the important words when signs are designed in a better way.

---

### Slide 05 — Research Questions 2 & 3

My second question is about attention. I divided each sign into different areas. I wanted to know which part of the sign gets the most attention. My third question is about how different parts of the sign work together to guide the eyes.

---

### Slide 06 — Research Pipeline (Timeline)

Here is my research plan. I started in November 2024 by finding the problem. Then I did fieldwork, created design rules, built tools, ran experiments in May 2026, and finally wrote this report in June. The whole project took about one and a half years.

---

### Slide 07 — Phase 1: Literature Review

First, I read many books and papers about sign design in museums and gardens. Three theories were very important. Tilden said signs should connect to visitors' personal experience. Ham said signs should be interesting and well-organized. Serrell said the layout and font size really matter.

---

### Slide 08 — Phase 2: Co-creation Workshops

Next, I held workshops with visitors, garden staff, and designers. Together we looked at old signs and talked about the problems. We found five main issues. The biggest ones are: small font size, too much information, no connection to daily life, too many difficult words, and no action guide.

---

### Slide 09 — Phase 3: A and R Design Principles

From these findings, I created three design rules called A/R/S. This slide shows A and R. "A" means Atmosphere — we should use questions to make visitors curious, not just give facts. "R" means Relevance — we should connect plants to things people know, like how a leaf feels or what a plant can be used for.

---

### Slide 10 — Phase 3: S Design Principle

"S" stands for Structure. The sign must be easy to scan. I used clear sections, large headings, and simple icons. Visitors should not face a wall of text. Instead, the sign is divided into small, readable blocks. This helps people find information quickly.

---

### Slide 11 — Phase 3: Sign Audit

I also checked all 70 old signs in the greenhouse. The result was surprising. Over 91 percent of the old signs completely missed the A and R points. They only had facts, but no interesting questions and no connection to daily life. This showed that we really needed to make changes.

---

### Slide 12 — Phase 4: Control vs Intervention

For the experiment, I made two kinds of signs. The control group used the old style — long paragraphs, no clear sections. The intervention group used my new A/R/S design — with big questions, sensory tips, and clear blocks. I wanted to compare how people look at each type.

---

### Slide 13 — Phase 4: Eye-Tracking System

To measure where people look, I built a simple eye-tracking system using a normal web camera. It does not need expensive equipment. Before reading the sign, visitors look at 9 points on the paper. The system then knows where their eyes are looking. This is a low-cost solution.

---

### Slide 14 — Heatmap Overview

Here are the heat maps from 9 participants. Red and yellow spots show where people looked the most. On the left side, you can see the old signs. On the right side, the new signs. Just by looking, you can tell the new signs brought more attention to the important areas.

---

### Slide 15 — Result 1: Reading Coverage Improved

Now the numbers. For the old signs, people only read about 38.6 percent of the text area. But for the new signs, the number went up to 45.3 percent. That is a 6.7 point increase. Six out of nine people showed clear improvement. This means the new design really helped people read more.

---

### Slide 16 — Result 2: R Principle Is the Key

The most important finding is about the R area, which means the sensory and use tips. In the first 10 seconds, attention to the R area jumped from only 5 percent to over 21 percent. For the whole reading time, it went from 8.5 percent to 35.1 percent. All nine people showed the same pattern. This means the R part is the strongest tool to keep people reading.

---

### Slide 17 — Conclusions

To conclude, this study has three key contributions. First, I built a complete method from finding problems to testing with real data. Second, the A/R/S design rules can be used not only in plant gardens but also in museums and science centers. Third, I showed that a low-cost eye-tracking system can produce useful academic data.

---

### Slide 18 — Future Work (Doctoral Research)

For my PhD, I want to go further. The next step is about memory — can people remember what they read after they leave the garden? I also want to study how signs work together, not just one by one. When visitors walk through a garden, the signs should form a clear path in their mind.
